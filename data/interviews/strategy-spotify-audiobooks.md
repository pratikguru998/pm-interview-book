Why did Spotify get into audiobooks? Was it a good idea?

::candidate 00:05
Let me structure this: (1) Spotify's strategic situation, (2) why audiobooks specifically, (3) how they did it, (4) evaluation — benefits, risks, and verdict, (5) how I'd measure success.

Quick clarification: should I evaluate it from Spotify's business perspective, or also from users and authors?

::interviewer
Mainly business, but users matter.

::candidate 00:25
**1. Strategic situation:**
- Spotify's core business, music, has **structurally thin margins**: a large share of revenue goes to labels and publishers as royalties. Spotify doesn't own the content and can't easily differentiate on catalogue (Apple Music has similar songs).
- It needs **higher-margin content** and reasons for users to stay and pay more.
- It already moved into podcasts for these reasons: owned or exclusive content, ad revenue.

**2. Why audiobooks:**
- **Same behaviour:** listening, often during commutes and chores; Spotify already owns that habit.
- **Large, growing market**, dominated by Audible, whose credit-based model frustrates some users.
- **Bundling power:** adding audiobook hours into Premium increases perceived value, which supports price increases and reduces churn.
- **Margin:** spoken-word content can have better economics than music, depending on publisher deals.
- **Strategic positioning:** be *the* audio app, not just a music app.

::candidate 01:20
**3. How they did it:** started with purchasing individual audiobooks, then included a monthly listening allowance (e.g. 15 hours) in Premium in some markets, and launched an add-on for more hours. They also used the recommendation engine to surface audiobooks to music and podcast listeners.

**4. Evaluation:**

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Increases Premium value → price increase power, lower churn | Publisher payments are expensive; bundle costs scale with usage |
| Higher-margin category diversifies away from labels | Audible is entrenched; serious audiobook fans may stay with it |
| Leverages existing distribution + recommendation strengths | Hour caps may feel limiting to heavy listeners |
| Cross-sell opportunities (authors, podcasts) | App complexity: music, podcasts, books in one interface |

::interviewer
So was it a good idea?

::candidate 02:10
I think **yes, strategically**, with the right success criteria. Spotify's biggest problem is dependence on music economics and lack of differentiation; audiobooks give a differentiated bundle and more pricing power. It's a smart use of an existing habit and huge distribution. The key is measuring it correctly: **not** standalone audiobook revenue, but the effect on **Premium retention, ARPU and gross margin**. Bundle economics also depend on usage distribution: most users listen to a few hours, so a cap protects costs while still adding perceived value.

**5. Metrics:**
- % of Premium users who try audiobooks; hours per audiobook listener.
- **Churn of audiobook listeners vs comparable Premium users** (with care for selection bias).
- ARPU change after price increases tied to added value; add-on purchase rate.
- Gross margin per user (audiobook costs vs revenue uplift).
- **Reversal criteria:** if costs exceed retention/ARPU uplift for 2+ years.

**Summary:** Spotify entered audiobooks to escape music's thin margins and differentiate its bundle, using its listening habit and distribution. I think it's a good strategic move if judged on Premium retention, pricing power and margin, not on beating Audible directly.
