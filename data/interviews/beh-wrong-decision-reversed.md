::interviewer 00:00
Tell me about a time you changed your mind based on new information.

::candidate 00:05
**Situation.** I was PM for the home screen of a news app. I was convinced that a cleaner, more visual redesign (bigger images, fewer stories per screen) would increase engagement. I had championed it with leadership based on design reviews and positive feedback from a small user panel.

**Task.** We A/B tested the redesign before rollout, and I owned the decision to ship it.

::interviewer
What happened in the test?

::candidate 00:35
**Action.**
1. **The results contradicted my belief:** overall sessions per user dropped **4%** in the treatment group after two weeks, significant.
2. **I resisted the urge to rationalize it** ("it's a novelty effect", "users just need time"). Instead, I checked the data across segments with our analyst.
3. **Segment findings:** **new users** engaged *more* with the redesign (+8% sessions). **Power users** engaged *less* (−10%): they relied on scanning many headlines quickly, and the bigger images meant more scrolling.
4. **I went back to leadership** and said plainly: "My hypothesis was partly wrong. The redesign helps new users but hurts our most loyal readers."
5. **New solution:** a **layout toggle** ("visual" vs "compact headlines"), with new users defaulting to visual and existing heavy users keeping compact, plus a simple way to switch.

::interviewer
And the outcome?

::candidate 01:20
**Result.** A follow-up test with the adaptive default + toggle increased overall sessions per user by **6%**, with no segment worse off. Power users kept their scanning workflow; new users got the friendlier design.

**Learning.** Strong opinions, weakly held. A failed test isn't a failure if you learn *why*: segmenting the results turned a negative result into a better product. I now pre-register which segments I'll check before a test, so I don't cherry-pick afterwards.
