::candidate 01:00
**Why consider it.** Netflix's leadership has often said its real competition is for *leisure time*: other streamers, YouTube, TikTok, and gaming. Gaming is one of the biggest entertainment categories by time and revenue, especially among teens and young adults, a group whose attention Netflix needs. And as subscriber growth slows in mature markets, retention and engagement become the growth levers.

::candidate 01:30
**Company: strengths and weaknesses.**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Hundreds of millions of paying members with billing in place | No game development culture or track record |
| Beloved IP (Stranger Things, Squid Game, Wednesday) | Games have hit-driven, uncertain economics |
| World-class recommendation and personalization | App is built for passive viewing; gaming discovery is weak |
| Brand and global distribution | Talent costs; studios are expensive |

**Customers.** Within the member base:
- **Casual mobile gamers** (huge, broad demographics): already play puzzle and narrative games.
- **Families and kids**: interested in games tied to shows.
- **Fans of specific IP**: want more time in those worlds.
- Hardcore console/PC gamers: loyal to existing platforms, demanding. **Not a good target.**

**Competition.**
- Mobile: free-to-play with ads and in-app purchases dominates. Subscription bundles like Apple Arcade exist but are niche.
- Console/PC: Xbox Game Pass, PlayStation, Steam. Giants with decades of expertise.
- Substitutes: YouTube, TikTok, social apps.
- Porter's view: intense rivalry, powerful creators (hit studios), low switching costs for players. **Making money from games directly is hard.**

::candidate 03:00
**Options:**
1. **Don't enter.** Focus on video, and maybe licence IP to game companies for royalties.
2. **Casual and narrative mobile games, bundled free in the subscription, no ads or in-app purchases**, built around Netflix IP plus licensed hits. Some studio acquisitions to build capability.
3. **Cloud gaming platform** for TV and devices, including AAA titles, competing with Game Pass.
4. **TV party games** played with phones as controllers (social, family).

::candidate 03:30
**Evaluate:**

| Option | Fit with objective (retention) | Ability to win | Cost / risk | Strategic value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Don't enter / licence | Low | n/a | Low | Royalty income, no engagement |
| 2. Bundled mobile games | **High**: adds perceived value; IP cross-promotion | Medium (IP + bundle advantage) | Medium | Builds capability gradually |
| 3. Cloud AAA | Medium | **Low** vs Microsoft/Sony | **Very high** | Risky, distracting |
| 4. TV party games | Medium–high (families) | Medium | Low–medium | Uses the TV screen Netflix already owns |

The key insight for option 2: **Netflix doesn't need games to make money directly.** If a game makes a member 5% less likely to cancel, that's worth a lot across hundreds of millions of members. Free-to-play competitors can't easily match "no ads, no in-app purchases, included in what you already pay."

::interviewer
But won't most members never play? How do you justify the investment?

::candidate 04:30
Probably most won't, and that's fine. Two points: (1) Like any bundle, value can be concentrated: if a meaningful minority plays regularly and their churn falls, the economics can work. (2) We need to *measure* it rigorously, comparing churn of players vs similar non-players using matched cohorts or holdouts, because players might just be more engaged members anyway (selection bias). So I'd set a clear bar: e.g. **players' retention must be meaningfully better than matched non-players within 18 months**, or we scale back.

::coach
Pushback on "most won't use it" is common. Accepting it, then explaining bundle economics and how you'd measure causality, scores high on business judgement and analytical rigour.

::candidate 05:20
**Recommendation: enter, carefully, with option 2 + 4.**
- **What:** casual, narrative and party games, tied to Netflix IP where possible, included in all plans with no ads or in-app purchases.
- **How (build/buy/partner):** licence proven games for breadth now; acquire a few small studios for capability; build IP games for big franchise launches (game launches alongside a new season).
- **Where:** mobile first (reach), then TV party games (family time, differentiated).

**First 3 steps:**
1. Launch a curated catalogue of ~20 licensed games + 2 IP games tied to the next big show season.
2. Build game discovery into the Netflix app (rows, "Play the game" on show pages).
3. Set up a rigorous measurement framework (holdouts) from day one.

**Metrics:**
- **Primary:** churn rate of game players vs matched non-players.
- Monthly active game players (% of members), game hours per player, show ↔ game cross-engagement (players watching more of the IP).
- **Kill criteria:** no meaningful retention lift after 18 months → reduce investment to IP licensing only.

**Risks:** poor game quality damaging the brand, low discoverability, talent costs. Mitigation: curation, fewer but better games, focus on IP.

::candidate 06:40
**Summary:** Yes, Netflix should enter gaming, but as a **retention play, not a gaming business**. Bundled, ad-free casual and IP-driven games increase the value of the subscription and compete for leisure time, without fighting console giants head-on. Success is measured by retention lift among players, with clear kill criteria.

::interviewer
Good. What would make you change your recommendation?

::candidate 06:55
Three signals: (1) the holdout shows no causal retention lift; (2) game development costs grow far beyond plan without engagement growth; or (3) a cheaper alternative, like interactive content or licensing IP to studios, produces similar engagement. Any of these would push me towards option 1: licensing.
