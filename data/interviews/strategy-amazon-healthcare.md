**Objective:** build a large, durable healthcare business that also strengthens Prime loyalty, over 5–10 years.

Structure: market attractiveness → Amazon's right to win → customer pains → options → evaluation → recommendation + risks + metrics.

::candidate 00:45
**Market attractiveness.** US healthcare is enormous (well over $4 trillion a year, roughly a sixth of GDP) and widely seen as inefficient: opaque pricing, long waits, poor consumer experience, fragmented records. Consumer expectations shaped by e-commerce aren't met. So the market is attractive, but complex: heavy regulation (HIPAA, licensing), powerful incumbents (insurers, hospital systems, PBMs), and payment flows controlled by insurers.

**Amazon's right to win:**
- **Logistics:** fast, reliable delivery (pharmacy).
- **Customer experience obsession** and huge trust in delivery and returns.
- **Prime base:** a large share of US households.
- **AWS:** health data infrastructure, AI.
- **Existing assets:** Amazon Pharmacy, One Medical (primary care), past experiments (Amazon Care ended, Haven ended). Lessons: going alone against systemic complexity is hard.

::candidate 01:50
**Customer pains (consumers):**
1. Long waits for primary care appointments.
2. Prescription hassle: pharmacy queues, refills, opaque pricing.
3. Chronic condition management (diabetes, hypertension): fragmented, reactive.
4. Confusing bills and costs.

**Options:**
1. **Double down on pharmacy:** transparent pricing, automatic refills for chronic meds, delivery, a Prime discount program (Amazon has offered RxPass).
2. **Integrated virtual-first primary care + pharmacy:** One Medical for virtual and in-person care → prescription → delivered.
3. **Chronic care management programmes:** devices (glucose monitors), coaching, meds, care team.
4. **Become an insurer / health plan.**
5. **B2B:** sell health benefits (care + pharmacy) to employers.

::interviewer
Why not become an insurer? That's where the money flows.

::candidate 02:35
It's tempting, but: (1) insurance is a capital- and regulation-heavy business with thin margins and actuarial expertise Amazon lacks; (2) it would make Amazon a direct competitor to the insurers it needs as partners for pharmacy and care; (3) the Haven experience showed how hard systemic change is. I'd rather **partner with insurers** and become the best front door for consumers, and keep the option to move into insurance later if Amazon builds enough care capability and data.

**Recommendation: build the "Prime Health" loop: care → prescription → delivery → follow-up.**
- **Now:** integrate One Medical virtual visits with Amazon Pharmacy for seamless prescription delivery, with transparent prices and automatic refills; Prime members get discounted membership.
- **Next:** chronic condition programmes (starting with diabetes and hypertension), combining connected devices, meds and care team check-ins.
- **Partner** with insurers and employers for coverage; avoid becoming an insurer for now.

**Metrics:**
- **North Star:** members actively using 2+ health services (care + pharmacy).
- Prescription retention (refill adherence), time to appointment, member NPS, Prime retention lift among health members.
- **Guardrails:** clinical quality outcomes, privacy incidents, **separation of health data from retail/advertising data** (trust is make-or-break).

**Summary:** Healthcare is huge and consumer-unfriendly, and Amazon's logistics and experience strengths fit pharmacy and virtual-first care best. I'd build an integrated care-to-delivery loop tied to Prime, then expand to chronic care, partnering with insurers rather than competing with them, and protecting trust through strict data separation.
